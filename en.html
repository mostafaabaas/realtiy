Guide 1 · Tour filming

How a unit walkthrough should be shot to sell the unit — not the compound's beauty shots

A walkthrough that sells is filmed as a buyer's walk, not an architecture commercial. Aerial lakes and landscaping make people admire the project — but what makes them book a visit is an honest tour inside a unit they can imagine living in. This is how we build it:

Walk in the order of a buyer's day

  • Start at the unit's front door — that is the first moment of any real visit, and the viewer decides there whether to keep watching. Do not open with a drone shot or a project logo.
  • The order is the order of living: entrance, living room, kitchen, bedrooms, bathrooms, then the balcony or terrace. One plain sentence per room about how it is actually used — not poetic descriptions.
  • Honest scale: real furniture or familiar-sized objects in frame, so the eye measures the space correctly. Ultra-wide lenses that inflate rooms buy views — and disappointing visits.
  • The practical things buyers actually ask about: storage, how the kitchen works, how many bathrooms serve whom, what the balcony fits, where the laundry goes. These are the questions deals close on.
  • Natural light and a real voice — film in daylight with the windows open, and let the creator talk the way they talk. Excess gloss signals "ad" and makes viewers discount every word.

And finish outside the unit

The last part of the tour is the unit's context: the project around it, the amenities that will genuinely be used, and where it sits in the city. A buyer is not buying square metres — they are buying what their day will look like. But keep the order straight: the unit first, the compound last — the reverse of most project videos, which is exactly why most of them do not sell.

Guide 2 · Off-plan launches

UGC for off-plan launches: build trust before there is a building

In off-plan, the product being sold is trust. The buyer is paying for a promise — so the content that works is not glossy renders, but a steady stream of proof that this developer actually delivers, and that this project is actually moving. Good launch content is built in five layers:

  • The delivered record first — creator tours inside projects the developer has already handed over: real units, real residents, real finishing. This is the strongest answer to "will they deliver what they promise?"
  • Construction progress, dated and repeated — the same filming angles on site every period, with the date visible. A regular progress series builds a credibility no single launch video can buy.
  • The show unit, honestly — film the show unit as a show unit, stating what is included in delivery and what is display only. Ambiguity here becomes complaints later.
  • Fear-question videos — every launch gets the same questions: when is delivery, really? What does the finishing look like? How do maintenance and services work? Short videos answering one question each, from people who visited the site themselves.
  • The published payment plan — and only that — the creator explains the official, announced payment plan exactly as published. No return promises, no "prices will rise", no investment talk. Content that crosses that line destroys the trust the whole launch stands on.

The render rule

Renders and designs have their place — explaining the concept and the design. But any render that appears in content is clearly labelled as a design, not footage. Blurring that line is the fastest way to kill a launch's credibility, and a buyer who discovers the blur on their own does not come back.

Guide 3 · Briefing

What to put in a creator brief for a real-estate tour

A real-estate tour brief is not a normal product brief — the creator will be speaking about sensitive financial and legal facts, so the brief has to draw the line precisely between what may be said and what may not. Its structure is five parts:

1 · Location facts

The district and area, the nearest landmarks and services, and distances and drive times exactly as the developer publishes them — the creator does not round a number or invent a location advantage that is not in the project's official materials.

2 · Unit facts

The area, the layout (bedrooms and bathrooms), floor and type, and the delivered finishing level — and if the tour is in a furnished show unit, the brief states plainly what in frame is included in delivery and what is not.

3 · Payment-plan facts: may say / may not say

  • May say: the official published payment plan (down payment, instalments, term), the announced delivery date, and any official launch offers currently running, with their dates.
  • May not say: any promise of investment return or guaranteed rent, price-appreciation predictions, "approximate" delivery dates of their own, or unit prices the developer has not approved in writing.

4 · Disclosure and filming rules

Paid-partnership disclosure appears in the content itself, per platform rules. Renders are labelled as renders. Location-access permits and any drone filming are production's responsibility and are arranged before the day — the creator never films in a place, or in a way, that was not permitted.

5 · Must-show moments and format

A complete tour in natural walking order, vertical format, the creator on camera or in voiceover per their own format, and context shots last. Deliverables and usage scope are agreed in writing before filming — number of videos, whether raw footage is included, and organic/paid use and its duration.

05 — FAQ

What developers and brokers ask before their first pack

Can creators film inside our compound or project?

Filming is arranged in writing before anything happens. The developer grants access to the agreed units and locations, and we coordinate the tour plan, the creators and the schedule — no filming takes place without clear permission, and no creator enters a place that was not agreed.

What can a creator say about payment plans and prices?

The creator states only the published facts in the brief: the official payment plan, the announced delivery date, and the unit's specifications. Strictly forbidden: promises of returns or price appreciation, invented delivery dates, or figures the developer has not approved — and that is written into the brief itself before filming.

Our project is still under construction (off-plan) — what would we film?

We film what builds trust before delivery: the developer's actually delivered projects, show units and real finishing, dated construction progress, and tours of the real site. Renders are clearly labelled as renders whenever they appear — no design is sold as reality.

Can we run the videos in our ads? What are the usage rights?

Usage scope is agreed in writing before production: where the videos may be used (your project pages, your organic channels, your paid ad accounts), on which platforms, and for how long. The usage scope you buy is the usage scope written in your agreement — and whitelisting through the creator's own handle is arranged per creator when your pack includes it.

How long does a creator tour batch take from agreement to delivery?

The exact schedule is confirmed in a written plan before production. It depends on the number of units and creators, location-access sequencing and creator availability — so the plan is built around your sales calendar and launch timing, not a generic promise.

Are prices published?

No prices are published. Packs are structured — a defined number of tours, videos and usage scope — and priced in a written quote after a free consultation or brief, based on the size of the project and the content you need.
